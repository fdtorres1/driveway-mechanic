# Brakes Grinding: What to Check First

Let's be direct: **if your brakes are grinding, this is more urgent than squealing.** That harsh, metallic, grating sound means the brake pad friction material is completely worn through and the metal backing plate is grinding directly against the rotor. You're literally destroying parts with every stop.

This isn't a "deal with it next weekend" situation. Grinding brakes mean:
- **Longer stopping distances** — you have significantly reduced braking power
- **Rotor damage is happening right now** — what could have been a $60 pad job is becoming a $200+ pad-and-rotor job
- **Caliper damage is possible** — if the rotor gets thin enough, the caliper piston can overextend

The good news? Even grinding brakes are a very manageable DIY repair if you catch them before caliper damage occurs. Let's assess what you're dealing with.

## What's Actually Happening

When brake pads wear down completely, the soft friction material is gone and you're left with the steel backing plate. This steel plate presses directly against the cast iron rotor when you brake. That's the grinding noise — **metal on metal.**

Here's why every mile matters:

- **Day 1-3 of grinding:** Rotors are getting scored (deep grooves). Rotor resurfacing might still save them.
- **Week 1-2:** Rotors are severely gouged. They need replacement now, not just resurfacing.
- **Beyond that:** Rotors can get dangerously thin, overheat, crack, or the caliper piston can push through the bore.

<Callout tone="warning">
**The math is simple:** Brake pads alone cost $30-$70 per axle. Pads + rotors cost $80-$200 per axle. But if you let grinding continue until caliper damage occurs, you're looking at $150-$400 for a caliper *on top* of pads and rotors. Stop driving on grinding brakes.
</Callout>

## Pads Only vs. Pads + Rotors: The Decision

This is the big question, and here's a simple rule:

**If the brakes have been grinding for more than a day or two, replace the rotors.** Period. The money you save trying to reuse scored rotors will come back as noise, vibration, and premature pad wear. New rotors are cheap insurance for a smooth, quiet brake job.

| Scenario | Recommendation | Cost (DIY, per axle) |
|----------|---------------|---------------------|
| Pads worn but rotors smooth | Pads only | $30-$70 |
| Light rotor scoring | Pads + rotor resurfacing | $60-$120 |
| Deep grooves or metal-on-metal | Pads + new rotors | $80-$200 |
| Caliper seized | Pads + rotors + caliper | $120-$300 |

## Prevention: How to Avoid Getting Here Again

- **Listen for squealing.** Grinding is what happens when you ignore squealing. The wear indicator squeal gives you 1,000-3,000 miles of warning — use that time to replace pads.
- **Inspect brakes every 15,000-20,000 miles** (or every other oil change). A 60-second visual check through the wheel spokes catches wear early.
- **Use quality pads.** Ceramic pads last longer and come with better wear indicators than bargain pads.
- **Don't ride the brakes.** Use engine braking on long downhills. Constant light braking generates heat that glazes pads and accelerates wear.
- **Replace both sides.** Always replace pads (and rotors) as a pair per axle. Mismatched wear causes uneven braking.

## Related Problems

If your brakes are **squealing but not grinding**, you've caught it earlier — check our [brakes squealing guide](/articles/brakes-squealing) for a less urgent approach.

If the **brake pedal pulsates or vibrates** when stopping, that's typically warped rotors from heat — see our [brake vibration guide](/articles/brake-vibration-pulsation).

If the **car pulls to one side when braking**, you likely have a stuck caliper or uneven pad wear on one side. This needs attention to prevent uneven tire wear and safety issues.
