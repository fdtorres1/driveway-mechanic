## 2-Ton vs 3-Ton: Which Capacity Do You Need?

This is the first question everyone asks, and the answer is simpler than you think.

**2-ton jacks** are fine for most sedans, compact cars, and smaller crossovers. A 2-ton (4,000 lb) rating means it can lift that much total — but you're only lifting one end of the car at a time, so even a 3,500 lb sedan is well within range. Two-ton jacks are also lighter and more compact, which matters if you're storing it in a tight garage.

**3-ton jacks** are the sweet spot for most home mechanics. They handle everything a 2-ton does, plus mid-size SUVs, trucks, and minivans without breaking a sweat. The extra capacity gives you a safety margin, and the price difference is usually only $20–$40. If you own a truck or plan to work on friends' vehicles, go 3-ton.

**The bottom line:** Unless you only own a Miata and will never touch anything bigger, get a 3-ton. The weight and size penalty is minimal, and you'll never have to wonder if you're too close to the limit.

<Callout tone="tip">
A jack's rated capacity is its **maximum** — not its ideal operating load. Running a 2-ton jack at 1.9 tons regularly will wear it out faster than a 3-ton jack lifting the same weight. Buy more capacity than you think you need.
</Callout>

## Low-Profile Jacks: Do You Need One?

Low-profile floor jacks have a minimum pickup height of around 3–3.5 inches, compared to 5–6 inches on standard jacks. You need one if:

- You drive a sports car, lowered vehicle, or anything with less than 4 inches of ground clearance
- You've tried to slide a standard jack under your car and it doesn't fit

For everyone else — stock-height sedans, trucks, SUVs — a standard jack works fine and usually costs less. Some jacks split the difference with a "low-profile" design that still reaches 18–20 inches, which is the best of both worlds if you can swing the price.

## Aluminum vs Steel: Weight vs Price

**Steel jacks** are heavy (60–90 lbs for a 3-ton), durable, and cheap. If your jack lives in the garage and never moves, steel is the practical choice. The weight actually helps — a heavier jack feels more planted and stable.

**Aluminum jacks** weigh roughly half as much (30–50 lbs for a 3-ton), which matters if you're loading it in and out of a truck bed or carrying it around. They also resist corrosion better. The trade-off is price — expect to pay 50–100% more for aluminum.

**Aluminum/steel hybrids** use aluminum for the body and steel for the heavy-duty contact points. They hit a nice middle ground on weight and price.

My take: if the jack stays in your garage, save the money and go steel. If you're mobile or your back complains about heavy lifting, aluminum is worth every penny.

## Floor Jack Maintenance Tips

Your floor jack is a hydraulic tool, and like all hydraulic tools, it needs occasional care:

- **Check the oil level** every 6–12 months. If the jack doesn't hold height or feels spongy, low hydraulic fluid is usually the culprit. Most jacks use standard hydraulic jack oil — check your manual.
- **Bleed air from the system** if the jack pumps but barely lifts. Open the release valve, pump the handle a few times with no load, then close the valve and test.
- **Store it in the down position** with the release valve slightly open. Storing it raised keeps the seals compressed and shortens their life.
- **Keep it clean.** Wipe dirt and grime off the saddle and ram. Debris works its way into the seals and causes leaks.
- **Inspect the wheels and casters.** Flat-spotted or seized wheels make the jack hard to position and can cause it to shift during use.

## FAQ

### Can I use a floor jack on my driveway?

Yes — as long as it's flat, paved, and in good condition. Concrete is ideal. Asphalt works but can dent under the jack's wheels in extreme heat. Avoid cracked or uneven surfaces.

### How often should I change my floor jack's hydraulic oil?

For home use, every 2–3 years is fine unless you notice performance issues. If the jack starts creeping down or won't reach full height, change the oil and bleed the system first.

### Is a bottle jack better than a floor jack?

They're different tools. Bottle jacks are more compact and powerful for their size, but they require a flat surface under the jack point and have a narrow saddle. Floor jacks are easier to position, more stable, and faster to operate. For garage work, a floor jack wins every time.

### Do I really need jack stands if I have a floor jack?

**Yes. Absolutely. Non-negotiable.** Floor jacks can fail — seals leak, release valves slip, hydraulic lines rupture. Jack stands are your insurance policy. A floor jack lifts the car; jack stands keep it from killing you.
