Brakes are the most critical safety system on your car. When those brake pads get thin or your rotors start pulsating, it's time to act — and replacing them yourself on a 2016–2023 Honda Civic is well within reach for anyone with basic wrench-turning skills.

This is an intermediate job. It's not as simple as an oil change, but it's not rocket science either. Honda made the 10th-generation Civic's brakes pretty accessible, and with the right tools, you can knock out a full pad and rotor replacement in about two hours — saving $250 to $400 compared to a shop.

Let's get into it.

## Step-by-Step: Replacing Front Brake Pads and Rotors

### 1. Prepare the Vehicle

Park on a flat, level surface. Engage the parking brake. Loosen (but don't remove) the lug nuts on both front wheels — about a quarter turn each. This is much easier while the wheels are still on the ground.

### 2. Lift and Secure

Position your floor jack under the front jacking point (the subframe crossmember) and lift the front end. Place jack stands under the frame pinch welds on each side. Lower the jack so the car rests on the stands. Give it a firm push to confirm stability. Remove the lug nuts and take the wheels off.

### 3. Remove the Caliper

Locate the two caliper slide pin bolts on the back of the caliper (14mm). Remove both bolts. The caliper will lift off the bracket. **Do not let the caliper hang by the brake hose** — use a bungee cord or wire hanger to hang it from the suspension spring or strut.

### 4. Remove the Caliper Bracket

With the caliper out of the way, remove the two caliper bracket bolts (17mm). These are the big bolts that attach the bracket to the steering knuckle. Set the bracket aside.

### 5. Remove the Old Rotor

The rotor should slide right off the hub. If it's stuck (they often are, thanks to rust), give it a few firm hits with a rubber mallet on the face of the rotor. You can also thread a bolt into the threaded hole on the rotor face to push it off the hub. Clean the hub surface with a wire brush to remove any rust.

### 6. Install the New Rotor

Wipe down the new rotor with brake cleaner to remove the oily anti-corrosion coating it ships with. Slide it onto the hub. You can hand-thread one lug nut to hold it in place while you work.

### 7. Clean and Grease the Caliper Bracket

Use a wire brush and brake cleaner to thoroughly clean the caliper bracket, especially the pad slide channels (the grooves where the pad ears sit). Apply a thin layer of brake caliper grease to the slide pins. Check that the slide pin rubber boots aren't torn — if they are, replace them.

### 8. Install New Brake Pads

Apply a thin layer of anti-squeal paste to the back of each pad (the metal side — never the friction side). Install the pads into the bracket — they should slide in and sit flat. The pad with the wear indicator clip goes on the inboard side (toward the engine).

### 9. Compress the Caliper Piston

The new pads are thicker than your worn ones, so you need to push the caliper piston back into its bore. Open the brake fluid reservoir cap under the hood (this prevents pressure buildup). Use a C-clamp or piston tool to slowly push the piston straight back until it's fully seated.

<Callout tone="tip">
  **Check your brake fluid level** before compressing the pistons. Pushing the pistons back forces fluid back into the reservoir. If the fluid level is already at the max line, siphon a little out first to prevent overflow. After installing new pads, the level will be back to normal.
</Callout>

### 10. Reassemble

Reinstall the caliper bracket and torque the bracket bolts to **80 ft-lbs**. Slide the caliper over the new pads and reinstall the slide pin bolts, torquing them to **37 ft-lbs**. Reinstall the wheel and hand-tighten the lug nuts.

### 11. Repeat on the Other Side

Do the exact same procedure on the opposite front wheel.

### 12. Final Steps

Lower the vehicle. Torque all lug nuts to **80 ft-lbs** in a star pattern. Before moving the car, **pump the brake pedal** 15–20 times until it feels firm. This seats the pads against the rotors. Check the brake fluid level and top off if needed with Honda DOT 3 brake fluid.

## Break-In Procedure (Bedding the Brakes)

New pads and rotors need to be properly bedded in for maximum stopping power. Here's how:

1. Find a quiet road with no traffic behind you
2. Accelerate to 35 mph, then apply moderate braking to slow to about 5 mph (don't come to a complete stop)
3. Repeat this 6–8 times
4. Then do 2–3 harder stops from 45 mph down to about 10 mph
5. Drive for 5–10 minutes at normal speed to let the brakes cool — avoid heavy braking during this time

<Callout tone="note">
  **Expect some noise and smell for the first 100 miles.** New brake pads go through a curing process. Slight odor and occasional light squealing is normal during break-in. If noise persists after 200 miles, inspect your work.
</Callout>

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Not cleaning new rotors.** New rotors ship with an oily anti-corrosion coating. If you don't clean it off with brake cleaner, you'll get poor braking and glazed pads. Wipe every rotor down before installation.
- **Letting the caliper hang by the hose.** This stretches and damages the brake hose internally. Always support the caliper with a bungee cord.
- **Forgetting to pump the brakes before driving.** After compressing the pistons, the brake pedal will go to the floor. You must pump it multiple times until it's firm before moving the car.
- **Skipping the break-in procedure.** Improperly bedded brakes will have reduced stopping power and may develop vibration. Take 15 minutes to bed them in properly.
- **Reusing worn slide pin boots.** Torn or cracked slide pin boots let water and dirt in, causing the caliper to stick. Inspect them every brake job and replace if needed.
- **Not re-torquing lug nuts.** After 50–100 miles of driving, re-torque your lug nuts. New rotors settle slightly and lug nuts can loosen.

## When to Replace Your Brakes

Keep an ear and eye out for these signs:

- **Squealing or grinding noise** when braking — the wear indicator is contacting the rotor
- **Brake pedal pulsation** — warped or unevenly worn rotors
- **Longer stopping distances** — pad material is worn thin
- **Visual inspection** — most pads are visible through the wheel spokes. If the pad material is less than 3mm (about the thickness of two pennies), it's time

Don't wait until metal is grinding on metal. At that point, you've gone from a $100 brake job to a $500+ repair with scored rotors, damaged calipers, or worse.

Replacing your own brakes is one of the most satisfying DIY car maintenance skills you can learn. You'll save hundreds of dollars each time, and you'll know the job was done right — because you did it yourself.
