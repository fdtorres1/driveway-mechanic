## Why Jack Stands Matter — A Reality Check

Here's what happens when a hydraulic floor jack fails: the release valve slowly leaks pressure, the car drops an inch per minute, and by the time you notice, a brake rotor is on your chest. Or the jack handle gets bumped, the release valve opens, and the car drops instantly. Or a seal blows and the ram drops under load.

These aren't hypotheticals. They're documented fatalities.

Jack stands are mechanical — there's no hydraulic fluid to leak, no seals to blow, no valves to bump. They're a steel post locked at a specific height. The only way they fail is if they're overloaded, improperly placed, or defectively manufactured. The first two are on you. The third is why you buy from reputable brands.

<Callout tone="note">
In 2020, Harbor Freight recalled **1.7 million jack stands** (Pittsburgh brand, 3-ton and 6-ton models) due to a ratchet pawl design flaw that could allow the stands to drop under load. This was later extended to additional models. If you own Harbor Freight jack stands from before 2021, check their recall page immediately. This recall was real, serious, and potentially fatal.
</Callout>

## Pin-Lock vs Ratchet Jack Stands

This is the single most important design decision in a jack stand, and the Harbor Freight recall made it a mainstream topic.

### Ratchet-Style Jack Stands

Ratchet stands use a toothed bar and a pawl (locking tooth) to set the height. You lift the saddle to the desired notch, and the pawl clicks into place. They're fast to adjust and easy to use one-handed.

**The problem:** If the pawl doesn't fully engage — due to a manufacturing defect, wear, or careless positioning — the stand can drop. The Harbor Freight recall was specifically about a pawl that could slip past the engagement point under certain conditions. Millions of these stands were in garages across America.

That said, well-made ratchet stands from reputable manufacturers are perfectly safe. The design itself isn't flawed — the execution on those specific Harbor Freight units was.

### Pin-Lock Jack Stands

Pin-lock stands use a solid steel pin inserted through a series of holes in the post to set the height. You lift the saddle, align the holes, and insert the pin. Some designs use a spring-loaded pin that automatically engages.

**The advantage:** There's nothing to slip. Either the pin is through the hole or it isn't. It's an inherently more fail-safe design because the locking mechanism is a solid piece of steel passing through two aligned holes.

**The disadvantage:** Height adjustment is slower, and you usually need two hands. The height increments are fixed — you can't fine-tune like you can with a ratchet.

### My Recommendation

For home mechanics, I lean toward **pin-lock designs** for the simple reason that they have fewer failure modes. But I wouldn't tell you to throw away a well-made ratchet stand from ESCO, Sunex, or another reputable brand. The key is buying quality and inspecting your stands regularly.

## 3-Ton vs 6-Ton: Which Rating Do You Need?

Jack stands are rated in pairs for total capacity, but each stand has an individual rating. When you see "3-ton jack stands," each stand is rated for 3 tons (6,000 lbs) — not 3 tons for the pair.

**3-ton (6,000 lb per stand):** Adequate for virtually all passenger vehicles. Even a full-size truck typically only puts about 2,500–3,000 lbs on one end when you lift it. Three-ton stands have a comfortable safety margin for sedans, SUVs, and light trucks.

**6-ton (12,000 lb per stand):** Overkill for most home mechanics, but appropriate if you work on heavy-duty trucks, large SUVs, or commercial vehicles. Also a good choice if you simply want the maximum safety margin and don't mind the extra size and weight.

**For most people:** 3-ton stands are the right call. They're lighter, more compact, and less expensive while still providing a massive safety margin for passenger vehicles.

<Callout tone="tip">
When using jack stands, always place them on the vehicle's designated support points — usually the pinch welds, frame rails, or specific reinforced areas listed in your owner's manual. Placing stands on suspension components, body panels, or random spots under the car is asking for trouble.
</Callout>

## ANSI/ASME PASE Certification: What It Means

After the Harbor Freight recall, the industry adopted the **ANSI/ASME PASE-2014** standard for portable automotive service equipment. Jack stands that meet this standard have been tested for:

- **Load capacity** at the rated weight
- **Stability** under load
- **Locking mechanism reliability** (pawl engagement, pin retention)
- **Material quality** and weld integrity

Not all jack stands on the market carry PASE certification — particularly cheap imports. While certification alone doesn't guarantee safety, it means the design has been independently verified to meet established engineering standards. All else being equal, buy certified.

## Jack Stand Don'ts

Let me be absolutely clear about what you should **never** do:

- **Don't stack jack stands** on blocks, wood, or other objects to gain height. If you need more height, buy taller stands.
- **Don't use a single stand.** The vehicle can pivot around one support point and fall. Always use pairs.
- **Don't place stands on soft ground.** Asphalt in extreme heat can deform. Concrete is the only reliable surface. If you must use asphalt, place a wide, thick steel plate under each stand.
- **Don't use damaged stands.** If a stand has been dropped from height, visibly bent, cracked, or has a damaged locking mechanism, retire it. Steel fatigues, and stands cost less than your hospital deductible.
- **Don't forget to shake-test.** Every single time. No exceptions. Three seconds of shaking can reveal a stand that isn't fully seated.

## FAQ

### How many jack stands do I need?

A minimum of two (one pair). If you need all four wheels off the ground (for exhaust work, suspension jobs, etc.), buy four. Having a spare pair is never a bad idea.

### Can I use jack stands on asphalt?

Technically yes, but with caution. Hot asphalt can deform under concentrated loads, causing stands to lean. Use wide steel plates under each stand base if you're working on asphalt in warm weather. Concrete is always preferred.

### How long do jack stands last?

Indefinitely, if they're not damaged or overloaded. Inspect them before each use — look for cracks, bent posts, worn locking mechanisms, and surface rust that might indicate weakened metal. Store them indoors to prevent corrosion.

### Should I buy 3-ton or 6-ton jack stands?

3-ton for most passenger vehicles. Even a heavy truck only puts about 3,000 lbs on one end. 6-ton gives you extra margin if you work on larger vehicles or simply want more confidence. The price difference is usually $20–$30, so if in doubt, go bigger.

### Were the Harbor Freight jack stands ever fixed?

Harbor Freight issued a recall and replacement program for the affected Pittsburgh jack stands (several model numbers from 2013–2020). Replacement stands feature a redesigned pawl. If you have pre-2021 Harbor Freight jack stands, check their recall page with your model number. When in doubt, replace them.

### Can I use ramps instead of jack stands?

Ramps are great for tasks where you don't need the wheels off — oil changes, exhaust inspections, etc. But they don't help when you need to remove a wheel (brake jobs, tire rotations, suspension work). Think of ramps as complementary to jack stands, not a replacement.
