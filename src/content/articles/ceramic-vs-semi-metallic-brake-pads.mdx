## What's Actually in These Pads?

### Ceramic Brake Pads

Despite the name, ceramic brake pads aren't made of the same ceramic as your coffee mug. They're a composite of ceramic fibers, bonding agents, filler materials, and sometimes small amounts of copper. The ceramic compound is engineered to provide consistent friction across a wide temperature range while producing very fine, light-colored dust.

Ceramic pads were developed as an improvement over organic (asbestos) pads and have become the default choice for most passenger vehicles manufactured in the last 15–20 years. If your car came from the factory with ceramic pads, replacing them with ceramics is usually the right call.

### Semi-Metallic Brake Pads

Semi-metallic pads contain 30–65% metal content — typically steel wool, iron powder, copper, or composite alloys mixed with friction modifiers and fillers. The high metal content gives them excellent heat conductivity, which means they transfer heat away from the rotor surface efficiently.

Semi-metallic pads were the standard before ceramics became mainstream, and they're still the preferred choice for heavy-duty applications, performance vehicles, and situations where maximum stopping power matters more than comfort.

## Head-to-Head Comparison

### Noise

**Ceramic wins.** This is the most immediately noticeable difference. Ceramic pads are significantly quieter — the ceramic compound dampens vibration that would otherwise create brake squeal. If you've ever cringed at high-pitched squealing every time you tap the brakes, ceramic pads are the cure.

Semi-metallic pads are inherently noisier, especially when cold. That metallic grinding sound during the first few stops on a cold morning? Classic semi-metallic behavior. They quiet down once they warm up, but they'll never match ceramics for silence.

### Brake Dust

**Ceramic wins again.** Ceramic pads produce significantly less dust, and the dust they do create is fine, light-colored, and easy to clean. Your wheels stay cleaner, longer.

Semi-metallic pads produce darker, heavier brake dust that clings to wheels and is harder to remove. If you care about clean wheels (and your wheels are exposed, not covered by hubcaps), ceramic pads will save you a lot of washing.

<Callout tone="tip">
If you drive a European vehicle (BMW, Audi, Mercedes, VW), you may notice more brake dust than usual regardless of pad type. European braking philosophy prioritizes outright stopping power over pad life, resulting in softer pad compounds that wear faster and dust more.
</Callout>

### Stopping Power (Cold)

**Slight edge to semi-metallic.** Semi-metallic pads generally have more initial bite — you feel the brakes grab immediately when you press the pedal. This is partly due to the higher friction coefficient of the metal-to-metal interface at moderate temperatures.

Ceramic pads have a slightly more progressive feel. The initial bite is softer, but they build friction smoothly as you increase pedal pressure. Most daily drivers prefer this because it feels more controlled and predictable.

### Heat Performance

**Semi-metallic wins clearly.** This is where semi-metallic pads justify their existence. The metal content conducts heat away from the friction surface efficiently, and semi-metallic compounds maintain their friction coefficient at higher temperatures.

If you're towing, driving down mountain grades, or doing anything that generates sustained high brake temperatures, semi-metallic pads resist fade better. Ceramic pads can experience fade (reduced stopping power) at extreme temperatures — not dangerous under normal driving, but a real limitation for heavy-duty use.

### Pad Life

**Ceramic wins.** Ceramic pads generally last longer — 50,000–70,000 miles in typical driving, compared to 30,000–50,000 miles for semi-metallic. The ceramic compound is harder and wears more slowly.

However, this comes with a trade-off: ceramic pads can be slightly harder on rotors over their lifespan. The difference is minor, but if you're replacing pads every 50K+ miles, you should be checking rotor thickness at each pad change.

### Rotor Wear

**Slight edge to ceramic.** Despite being harder, ceramic pads are designed to wear in a way that's rotor-friendly. Semi-metallic pads, with their metal content, can be more abrasive on rotor surfaces — particularly cheaper semi-metallic pads with higher concentrations of rough metal particles.

In practice, the difference in rotor wear between quality ceramic and quality semi-metallic pads is small. Cheap pads of either type will eat rotors faster.

### Cold Weather Performance

**Semi-metallic wins.** Semi-metallic pads perform better in extreme cold. The metal content provides reliable friction even when the pads haven't warmed up. Ceramic pads can feel slightly grabby or inconsistent in very cold temperatures (below 20°F) until they reach operating temperature — usually after the first few stops.

If you live in a region with brutal winters, this is worth considering.

### Price

**Semi-metallic is cheaper.** A quality set of semi-metallic pads typically runs $25–$45 per axle, while ceramic pads cost $35–$70 per axle. Premium ceramic pads from brands like Akebono or Bosch can reach $50–$80.

Factor in the longer lifespan of ceramics, though, and the cost-per-mile difference narrows considerably.

## When to Choose Ceramic

Ceramic brake pads are the right choice if:

- **You're a daily commuter.** Quiet, clean, and long-lasting — exactly what you want for a vehicle that shuttles you to work and back.
- **You hate brake dust.** If clean wheels matter to you, ceramic is the clear winner.
- **Your car came with ceramic pads from the factory.** The braking system was designed around the friction characteristics of ceramic pads. Stay with what works.
- **You want the lowest maintenance option.** Longer pad life means fewer replacements.
- **Noise bothers you.** If you cringe at brake squeal, ceramics are dramatically quieter.

## When to Choose Semi-Metallic

Semi-metallic brake pads make more sense if:

- **You tow regularly.** The superior heat dissipation is a real safety advantage when hauling a trailer.
- **You drive in mountains.** Long downhill grades generate sustained heat that can fade ceramic pads.
- **You drive a heavy vehicle.** Full-size trucks and large SUVs benefit from the higher friction coefficient.
- **You live in extreme cold.** Semi-metallic pads perform more consistently in freezing temperatures.
- **Budget is the primary concern.** Semi-metallic pads cost less per set, even if the per-mile cost is similar.
- **Your car came with semi-metallic pads.** Many trucks and performance vehicles are spec'd for semi-metallic from the factory.

<Callout tone="note">
**What about organic pads?** Organic (NAO — Non-Asbestos Organic) pads are the softest, quietest, and cheapest option. They work fine for light-duty driving but wear the fastest and offer the least stopping power. They're increasingly rare as OEM equipment. For most people in 2026, the choice is between ceramic and semi-metallic.
</Callout>

## Can You Switch Between Types?

Yes — with caveats. You can generally swap from semi-metallic to ceramic or vice versa without any modifications. The calipers, rotors, and hardware are the same. However:

- **Bed-in procedure is critical.** New pads need to be properly bedded (broken in) by performing a series of moderate stops to transfer an even layer of pad material to the rotor surface. If you switch pad types without properly bedding the new pads, you can get vibration, noise, and uneven braking.
- **Rotor condition matters.** If your rotors were worn in by semi-metallic pads and you're switching to ceramic, having the rotors resurfaced (or replacing them) gives the ceramic pads a fresh surface to bed into. This isn't strictly required, but it improves results.
- **Check your vehicle's recommendations.** Some vehicles — particularly high-performance cars and heavy trucks — are specifically designed for one type. Check your owner's manual or consult a parts specialist.

<Callout tone="tip">
**The bed-in procedure for new pads:** Drive at moderate speed, then brake firmly (not emergency-stop hard) from 35 mph to 10 mph. Repeat 6–8 times with 30-second cooling intervals between stops. Then drive for 5 minutes with minimal braking to cool the pads. This transfers an even friction layer and prevents hot spots.
</Callout>

## What About Drilled and Slotted Rotors?

Both ceramic and semi-metallic pads work with drilled, slotted, or drilled-and-slotted rotors. The rotor design affects heat dissipation and gas venting, not pad compatibility. That said:

- **Drilled rotors + ceramic pads** is a popular combination for daily drivers who want reduced dust and improved wet-weather performance.
- **Slotted rotors + semi-metallic pads** is the go-to for heavy-duty and performance applications where maximum heat management is the priority.

For most daily drivers on stock rotors, the pad choice matters more than the rotor design.

## The Verdict

For the vast majority of people reading this — daily drivers with sedans, crossovers, or light SUVs — **ceramic brake pads are the better choice.** They're quieter, cleaner, last longer, and provide perfectly adequate stopping power for normal driving conditions. The Akebono ProACT is our top pick, with the Bosch QuietCast as a value alternative.

If you tow, drive a heavy truck, or live in extreme conditions, **semi-metallic pads are the smarter pick.** The Wagner SevereDuty line handles heavy-duty use better than any ceramic at the price.

And whatever you choose — ceramic or semi-metallic — please bed them in properly. Five minutes of break-in stops gives you thousands of miles of confident braking.
